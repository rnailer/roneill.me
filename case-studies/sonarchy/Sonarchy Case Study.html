04.1

A party game I designed three times, and what a room of strangers taught me

Sonarchy is a music party game for one speaker and a room full of phones. Someone sets a category, everyone finds a song that fits it, the songs play one by one, the room races to name each track, then rates how well it fit, out of ten, with a name on every score. I designed it screens first. I rebuilt it system first. Then I watched twenty-four strangers play it in a Dublin bar and redesigned it a third time. This is v3, and how it got here.

01Origin

It started around a table

Sonarchy began as a family game. Drinks, food, a Bluetooth speaker outside, everyone digging through their phones. Someone would call a category, love songs, songs to cry to, songs that should never have been hits, and we'd take turns playing our picks. Then the argument.

The fun was never the music. It was the gaps. My parents' love songs were never going to match the grandkids' picks, and the cheesy ones landed differently depending on who was at the table. Three generations, one speaker, and a running dispute about taste. When I did an AI course at TU Dublin in October 2025 and started building apps, that was the obvious thing to make real. It took three goes.

The premise

One phone plays the music. Everyone else picks, guesses and rates from their own phone. The kitchen table, with timers and a scoreboard.

02v1 & v2

Twice before: screens first, then the system

v1 was designed the way a designer designs: screen by screen in Figma, then built. The first family test broke three assumptions in twenty minutes. Showing the video pulled everyone's eyes off each other. The guessing, the best bit of the table version, had quietly disappeared. And ranking songs by dragging them onto a leaderboard was precise, fiddly, and nobody enjoyed it. None of that was visual. It was a system I had never written down.

So for v2 I kept the idea, threw away every line of code, and wrote for three weeks before opening an editor: a product spec, a technical spec, scoring rules, what happens when a song won't play. The most useful thing in those documents was an order. One phone is the jukebox, the screen, the referee and the source of truth, and half its limits aren't mine to change, so I ranked them and let the ranking settle every later argument. The drag became a 1 to 10 slider, the guess came back, and the score reveal became an attributed moment: you see exactly who gave you a four.

01

YouTube's rules

Breaking them ends the product, not a feature. Search and playback live inside the terms.

02

Audio physics

The host phone is the speaker source. No mechanic survives half a second of Bluetooth lag.

03

Fairness

The host's phone shows the title, so the host never guesses. It's the one I control, so it bends to fit.

v2 shipped to the App Store and the web, passed every check I could write, and played well with friends and family. Which is exactly when you need people who don't know you.

03The night

Twenty-four strangers, eleven games, one bar

On 15 October 2026 I ran the first public playtest: a Solo Meets Social night with Yinyang Events, at the Chelsea Drugstore on George's Street. I printed host sheets so any table could run a game without me, feedback cards so people could be honest without saying it to my face, and set myself one rule: don't explain the game unless someone is genuinely stuck. Every question is a design problem I haven't solved yet.

The game held. Twenty-four people played eleven games across the tables. The round timers ran on time, every phone's clock agreed, and the argument about taste that started at the kitchen table started again, this time between strangers.

Then, about an hour in, song search stopped. For everyone, at once. YouTube's Data API has a second, separate cap that only counts searches: 100 a day. I'd been approved for a large daily budget of API units and was nowhere near it. The 100-search cap was the limit nobody had read, me included. Every game still running ended within minutes.

What the room showed me

The system held. The waiting didn't. Between your turns, the game gave you nothing to watch.

The organiser had asked for it before the night: shorter games, around six players, more turns, less waiting around. Earlier rehearsals had said the same thing more quietly: interest faded across a full game. On the night it was plain. When it wasn't your song, your guess or your rating, your phone was a waiting room, and phones are very good at offering somewhere else to be.

The v3 brief came out of that. Make the waiting part of the show. Make every screen readable from across a table, in bad light, at a glance. Give bigger groups a way to play together instead of queueing. And give the people who don't win something better than a list.

04Direction

Three directions, one broadcast

I explored three full directions: A, the existing look calmed down; B, a sticker book; and C, a broadcast. Each was drawn across the same set of real screens rather than a moodboard, so they could be compared like for like on the screens that matter most.

C won because it reads from across a table. A black field. A seven-bar colour spine taken from the SMPTE test bars the brand already used. Display type in Syncopate caps, numbers in Space Mono, square corners everywhere. Casey and Walker stay, but they present from the corner instead of decorating every surface. And colour stops being decoration: each one means one thing.

My first build of C was a theme pass: new colours and type on the old components. It was quick, it was cheap, and I rejected it on sight. It was the old app in new paint. The layouts were still v2's answers to v2's problems. So every screen was rebuilt from a small kit of parts (spine, label, headline, number, button, tag, a character that knows which way to face) and checked side by side against its Figma frame at phone width before I signed it off.

Rating

One question, one big number, one slider from awful to banger. The clock is yellow because it's yours.

Standings

The round break became a race: everyone on a track, gaps you can read at a glance, and who leads by how much.

05The round

Six beats. One round.

v3 explains itself in one page instead of a stepped tutorial, and that page became the spine of the design. Six beats, each with one job, each readable in a glance.

01

The waiting is the show

While a song plays, every phone shows a live feed: who's guessing, who's in, who's not yet. The first correct guess is announced to the whole room, not just the winner.

02

A blank box, with help

The category is typed, with a presets sheet and SURPRISE ME for when your mind goes blank. More creative, and faster than scrolling a list.

03

Less dead air

The host can jump 20 seconds past a long intro, and add 30 seconds once the guessing closes. Short rooms, more turns.

04

Losing gets a moment

A podium for the top three, and a "you placed 2nd" beat on every other phone, so finishing fourth still ends on your own screen.

05

Ties get a face-off

Sudden death: a random category drawn in three seconds, one song each, and everyone rates.

06

Teams for big tables

The game deals teams, one captain picks the team's song each round, and scoring is team totals plus one MVP line.

06Ship

Built in the dark, flipped in one afternoon

A redesign this size can't ship as one big bang to a live game, so it didn't ship at all until it was finished. Every v3 screen went onto the main branch behind a switch that only staging and the test rig could see. Live players saw nothing change for three weeks, while bug fixes kept going straight to them. The rig ran every check against both designs, so v3 couldn't break v2 and neither could quietly drift.

On 6 November I checked nobody was mid-game, pushed, and production was serving v3 28 seconds later. The iOS release went out the same day. For seven days, adding ?ui=current to the address brought back the old design for anyone who needed it. The rollback was rehearsed and never used.

How v3 shipped

Then the terminal captured every screen from the release build, 93 of 95, into Figma, replacing the design file's frames with what actually ships. New design work now starts from a real screen.

I'm open about how it was built. The design calls, product decisions and copy are mine. The code was written with Claude, argued out against specs I wrote first, and checked by a Playwright rig that now runs over 1,800 checks per browser engine. AI makes code cheap, and cheap code is a liability unless something is checking it. The rig can tell me a button exists. It can't tell me the room got bored. That's what the night out was for.

Stack React + TypeScript (Vite) · Supabase Realtime (phase state machine, server-only writes) · YouTube Data + IFrame APIs · Capacitor (iOS) · RevenueCat · Vercel · Playwright (Chromium + WebKit, both designs)
07Decisions

The non-obvious calls

01

Broadcast over sticker book

The sticker book was more charming. The broadcast was more legible across a table in a dark bar. A party game is read at arm's length, in bad light, mid-argument.

02

Rebuild, don't repaint

The theme pass would have shipped in days. It also would have kept every layout decision v3 existed to undo.

03

A captain, not a vote

Team voting sounds fair and adds a wait to every round. One captain per round, rotating, keeps the pace and still spreads the choice.

04

Ship dark, flip once

No half-redesigned app in front of players. One flip, a seven-day way back, and a rollback rehearsed before it was needed.

08Reflection

The room is still the product

v1 taught me to plan the system before the pixels. v2 taught me the system can be right and the room can still drift. v3 is designed for whoever isn't holding the turn: the people watching, waiting, losing. That's most of the room, most of the time.

And a night out finds what a test rig can't. The rig checked every property I knew to ask for. The bar found a limit buried in someone else's quota page, and a boredom no test would ever fail on.

Lessons learned
01

Design the waiting, not just the turn

In a group game most players are idle most of the time. That idle time is the experience.

02

Read every limit

Not just the one you applied for. The cap that ended the night was on a different line of the same page.

03

Design from what ships

Figma now holds captures of the release build. Mockups that drift from the product are how v1 happened.
